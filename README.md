# Citibike Phase 4
This is a basic map that aims to use land use and biking data to determine where Citibike should expand next.

## Purpose
The purpose of this map is to identify areas as potential candidates for future expansions of the Citi Bike network. This map does so by using a combination of land use, bike ridership data, and bike route information to demonstrate which areas of New York City have both the density, trip demand, and available infrastructure to facilitate the placement of new stations in neighborhoods which do not currently have access to the bike share network.

## Scope and Limitations
The scope of this map is limited to basic land use and bicycle indicators within the boundaries of New York City. 

This map does <b>not</b> take into account several factors, including future land use and zoning initiatives, future bike network infrastructure expansion, per-station subsidy, and site-specific elements that would determine suitability for station location, or political factors. The map shows the locations of subway stations, but I have not performed analysis on the density of those stations within zip codes and their relationship to overall travel choice. The data sources for this represent static points in time and are <b>not</b> indicative of trends. 

## Context and Planning Rationale
Citi Bike is the largest bike share network in the US and one of the largest in the world, a peer of larger systems throughout the world such as those located in France and China. Since its introduction in 2013, the network has served millions of riders making a variety of trips; what started as a Bloomberg-era emissions reduction initiative has since cemented itself as an emergent aspect of the city's transportation system. Ridership has [surged](https://www.amny.com/nyc-transit/citi-bike-in-nyc-boroughs-ridership/), with trends making it clear that Citi Bike is not just a convenient option to get around, but has a clear role to play as public transportation.

This map is being published at a time when belt-tightening is taking place throughout the country at all levels of government. A combination of global economic crises, antagonistic money-granting administrations, and rising cultural apprehension makes the expansion of public transportation more polarizing and contentious than ever. At the same time, expanding mobility and accessibility of places for urban residents is more important than ever, as they are tied to public health, economic opportunity, and social cohesion. 

It is unclear how much money was spent during Citi Bike's Phase 3 and 3.5 expansions, and what combination of public and private money went into the mix. Previous stakeholders have [claimed](https://nyc.streetsblog.org/2019/07/17/mayor-i-want-to-do-citi-bike-on-the-cheap-and-ill-try-to-keep-riders-safe) that expanding the system to cover all five boroughs would cost $500-600 million dollars. On the surface, this is an eye-watering figure but it [pales](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2023-02-23/in-nyc-subway-a-case-study-in-runaway-transit-construction-costs) in comparison to other transportation expansion projects in the city. Meanwhile, the Citi Bike system consistently [shatters](https://www.instagram.com/p/DNGdl3-gGVR/?img_index=1&utm_source=envoie%20courriel&utm_campaign=732817e28c-Newsletter%20novembre%202023%20EN%20employ%C3%A9s_COPY_01&utm_medium=email&utm_term=0_bbadbef63b-732817e28c-236849074) ridership records and has a [healthy daily ridership](https://mot-marketing-whitelabel-prod.s3.us-east-1.amazonaws.com/nyc/March-2026-Citi-Bike-Monthly-Report.pdf), supporting a variety of trips ranging from commutes to errands to recreation. While Citi Bike is the nation's [most expensive](https://www.ibo.nyc.gov/assets/ibo/downloads/pdf/infrastructure/2025/2025-november-citi-bike-lessons-for-the-future-of-nycs-bike-share.pdf) bike share system, the cost effectiveness per rider-mile (even with subsidies) cannot easily be dismissed.

This makes expanding the Citi Bike network a compelling investment: relatively low capital costs and operating subsidies, quick installation of infrastructure, and minimal impacts on parking and curb space, with ready ridership. New York City gets to reap benefits similar to those of other public transportation expansions: fewer car trips, less emissions, safer streets, and healthier, better-connected populations. New users also benefit by having new options first-mile/last-mile connections to and from important destinations like mass transit stops, commercial corridors, neighborhood services, open and recreational spaces, and more. These benefits speak to the purpose of this map as outlined above: by using data, it is possible to plan the expansion of the Citi Bike network in such a way that targets the communities which would benefit the most from such an expansion.

I use two principal documents to guide my analysis and conclusions. The first is [NACTO's Bike Share Station Siting Guide](https://nacto.org/publication/bike-share-station-siting-guide/), which contains guidelines for where to locate bike sharing stations, and [ITDP's Bikeshare Planning Guide](https://itdp.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/05/2018-Bikeshare-Planning-Guide.pdf), which expands on the principles in NACTO's guide and includes other important elements like financing and political processes. In addition to these two documents, I aimed to use general transportation planning principles with capital and operation costs in mind, especially maintenance and per-rider subsidies. This mirrors the path of Citi Bike's introduction and expansion in New York City: first installed in the core Manhattan Business Districts, before expanding similar districts in Brooklyn and then expanding to more residential and commercial locations throughout the four boroughs. I want to emphasize what I said earlier in the Scopes and Limitations section above: while I am using land use and bike trip demand as proxies for cost feasibility, such factors are not explicitly considered by my analysis.

## Data and Methodology
My main datasets for this map are [PLUTO](https://www.nyc.gov/content/planning/pages/resources/datasets/mappluto-pluto-change) and [NYCDOT's Citywide Mobility Survey](https://www.nyc.gov/html/dot/html/about/citywide-mobility-survey.shtml) (2024).

I used PLUTO's ```BuiltFAR``` field as a general indicator of land use density, where a higher value implies higher land use density. I joined this data to a [map of Zip Code Tabulation Areas](https://www.census.gov/cgi-bin/geo/shapefiles/index.php?year=2020&layergroup=ZIP%20Code%20Tabulation%20Areas) from the US Census as a general geography representing neighborhoods, and then wrote a python script which calculated the average Built FAR for each ZCTA. I originally intended to use Neighborhood Tabulation Areas (NTAs), but for some reason PLUTO lacks that one geography. Needless to say I now have a bone to pick with some of my DCP colleagues. However, ZCTAs and NTAs are similar in size and coverage so I find it works for the purposes of this map. I included land use data for the entire city to ensure proper categorization of the data for analysis purposes outside of the Citi Bike coverage areas.

I used the [NYCDOT Citywide Mobility Survey User Guide](https://www.nyc.gov/html/dot/downloads/pdf/2024-cms-user-guide.pdf) to find the fields for all bicycle trips. Unfortunately, the only geography defined in the Citywide Mobility Survey is the Survey Zone. Survey Zones are [far larger](https://data.cityofnewyork.us/Transportation/Citywide-Mobility-Survey-Survey-Zones/sx7h-iavd/about_data) than ZCTAs. I wrote a python script to calculate the total number of all bicycle trips within each Survey Zone, and used spatial interpolation to distribute those trips among the smaller ZCTAs based on the area of each respective ZCTA. I left out the bike trips within the Citi Bike coverage areas because my analysis is more interested in conditions outside of the areas, and the categorization of bike demand data does not play as large a role. A clear next step for this analysis is to normalize distributed bike trips by population and use the trip weights provided by the dataset and explained in the User Guide appendix tables.

I also included [New York City Bike Routes](https://data.cityofnewyork.us/dataset/New-York-City-Bike-Routes-Map-/9e2b-mctv) to demonstrate examples of bike infrastructure deployed throughout the city. The nature of bike infrastructure planning in New York City is such that bike share planning and bike facility planning are closely linked, so it is difficult to disentangle the relationship between the two for the purposes of this analysis. However, the presence of bike infrastructure serves to help indicate areas where expansion of Citi Bike can be supported by existing facilities, making expansion more compelling (and anticipating community complaints about a "lack of infrastructure," not that they don't also complain when that infrastructure is installed in the first place).

I used the most recent complete month's csv file from [Citi Bike's System Data](https://s3.amazonaws.com/tripdata/index.html) to create a map of all Citi Bike stations. I used this map to quickly create a map of ZCTAs not covered by the Citi Bike network in QGIS: analyzing where Citi Bike stations touch ZCTAs within NYC, reversing the selection, and deleting the features captured by the inverted selection.

I structured my data in a way that intends to highlight the most obvious candidates for Citi Bike network expansion, both by calling attention to the areas with an animated border, and by indicating which areas are eligible using popups.

## Results
This map shows that Southern Brooklyn and Central Queens (especially Jamaica) are primed for Citi Bike expansion. The combination of strong land use density (augmented in many areas by recent and upcoming zoning initiatives as well as by City of Yes) and healthy bike ridership demonstrate that neighborhoods in those ZCTAs could benefit from bike share. From an equity perspective, it is especially important that areas without reliable access to subways or frequent buses be seriously considered for expansion. This would enable residents to travel to and from mass transit stations on their own terms, and ideally displace private auto travel done for smaller trips like errands or accessing neighborhood services. This is especially the case in the east of the Bronx and Queens, and in neighborhoods like Bensonhurst and Gravesend in Brooklyn where residents can find themselves to be a long walk from mass transit.
